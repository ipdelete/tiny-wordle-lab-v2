# Part IV: Learning through interaction

Parts I through III treat Wordle primarily as a model-training problem.

Part I teaches the mechanics of supervised fine-tuning, LoRA, distillation,
reward design, and GRPO. Part II asks why those methods did not yet produce a
reliable policy, then tests data, representation, and target changes under
controlled conditions. Part III tests whether the answer-only action space is
limiting the policy and whether a larger legal-guess vocabulary is both useful
and learnable.

Part IV changes the abstraction.

The model is no longer evaluated only as a function that maps a serialized
Wordle history to a next-word completion. It is a policy acting over a sequence
of state transitions:

```text
observation -> policy -> guess -> Wordle environment -> feedback -> observation
```

A guess changes the history that the policy must handle on the next turn. A
poor early guess does more than receive a poor score. It moves the model into a
state that may be rare or absent in expert-generated training data.

The core question for Part IV is:

> What changes when a Wordle policy learns from the states created by its own
> actions, rather than only from expert-generated examples?

This is a plan for later work. It does not claim that Part III has already
produced a better full-action policy, that a value model exists, or that
trajectory RL will improve the project. Each claim must be tested in the order
defined below.

## Why this part exists

The project began from an LLM post-training sequence:

```text
dataset -> SFT -> LoRA -> distillation -> GRPO
```

That was the right way to teach the basic techniques. In particular,
completion-level GRPO makes sense for tasks whose main structure is:

```text
prompt -> completion -> verifier -> reward
```

Lab 12 used that shape. It compared completed next-guess responses against a
reward function. This is a valid GRPO formulation, but it does not run the
chosen guess through subsequent Wordle turns.

Wordle is also a control problem:

```text
history -> guess -> feedback -> updated history -> next guess
```

The repository already contains most of the underlying mechanics:

* `tiny_wordle.game` scores guesses and filters candidates;
* `tiny_wordle.expert` has a deterministic symbolic teacher over the current
  answer action space;
* `tiny_wordle.benchmark` runs multi-turn model games;
* Labs 18b through 18d established that answer-constrained ranking reveals
  useful state-conditioned behavior while leaving strategic and late-game
  failures.

Lab 30 used these pieces for a narrow, supervised correction experiment. They
are not yet a canonical training environment. Part IV gives them one explicit
contract for reset, action validation, transition, reward, termination, and
trace persistence.

The relevant change is not "add an RL acronym." It is to let the policy create
the states from which it later learns.

## SAO background

[Single-Rollout Asynchronous Optimization for Agentic Reinforcement Learning
(SAO)](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.07508v1) is the motivating paper for the
last part of this course.

The paper contrasts group-based rollout methods such as GRPO with
single-rollout learning. Group-relative methods collect several rollouts from a
shared prompt before comparing their rewards. SAO instead uses individual
trajectories and a learned value model to estimate advantage. The paper treats
single-rollout variance, critic quality, policy lag, and asynchronous data
collection as central problems rather than implementation details.

Its action-observation sequence maps cleanly onto Wordle:

```text
a0 = guess 1
o0 = Wordle feedback 1
a1 = guess 2
o1 = Wordle feedback 2
```

The paper's skip-observation advantage discussion is especially useful here.
Wordle feedback is emitted by the environment, not generated by the policy.
Part IV will make that boundary explicit when it introduces temporal credit
assignment.

SAO is not the starting implementation target. Its distributed and
asynchronous systems work is unnecessary for a 0.6B model on one Mac. Part III
first tests whether model-created states are more useful than label- and
update-matched static expert data. Part IV then asks whether value learning,
actor-critic updates, or simulator-based GRPO can improve on expert correction.
A bounded SAO-style study comes later and must earn its complexity by beating
simpler baselines under a matched compute budget.

## Entry criteria

Part IV starts from the best frozen supervised policy available after Parts II
and III. "Best" is an experimental decision, not a name assigned in advance.
The entry record must state:

* the checkpoint and seed;
* the training corpus and its manifest;
* the state representation and prompt template;
* the action vocabulary used by the decoder;
* whether decoding is free, answer-constrained, or constrained to a larger
  legal-guess vocabulary;
* the evaluation decoder and, when RL is involved, the separate stochastic
  training policy;
* held-out answers and all exclusion boundaries;
* full-game, action-level, and failure-mode measurements.
* the Part III Lab 30 arm definitions, traces, and result.

Part III may find that its full-action teacher is valuable, neutral, or too
hard for the model to learn. Part IV must use the action space that the Part III
evidence supports. It must not assume that all 12,972 original legal guesses
are the deployed action space.

The Part III capstone decides the first Part IV question. Only a result that
passes its pre-registered Lab 30 gate permits Lab 33's iterative imitation
loop. A null or harmful result does not prove that RL cannot help, but it makes
policy-gradient work lower priority until the current supervised or interface
bottleneck is explained.

## The environment contract

Part IV separates data that the policy may observe from values held by the
environment, teacher, or evaluator.

| Layer | Contains | May enter the policy prompt? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hidden environment state | answer, transition internals, terminal truth | No |
| Policy observation | declared history, remaining turns, and any explicitly approved public derived features | Only this layer |
| Action contract | current allowed-guess vocabulary, decoder, action parser | Yes, through the declared decoder |
| Teacher and evaluator | candidates, expert choices, entropy, regret, target values, held-out labels | No, unless a separate representation experiment explicitly exposes a public derived feature |

The simulator may compute a candidate set from public history. Whether the
policy receives the exact set, a count, or neither is a representation choice.
Every experiment must name that choice. It cannot quietly become available
because the environment can compute it.

The first environment adapter must provide:

```python
observation = env.reset(answer, policy_view)
observation, reward, done, info = env.step(action)
```

`info` may contain answer-dependent diagnostics for the trainer and evaluator,
but it must not be passed back into the policy prompt unless the observation
contract says it is public.

The adapter must persist an immutable trace for every episode:

```text
episode_id
policy_checkpoint
policy_view
evaluation_decoder
training_policy
action_vocabulary_digest
tokenizer_version
eos_convention
restriction_algorithm
temperature
sampling_seed
answer_split
answer_id
turn
history_before
action
normalized_action_log_probability
per_token_log_probabilities
feedback
reward
terminal_reason
teacher_diagnostics
```

The raw answer may be excluded from model-facing artifacts, but the evaluation
artifact must retain a protected identifier that permits split and leakage
checks.

## Reward and action rules

The first trajectory experiments use sparse game outcome:

```text
solved within six legal turns: 1
otherwise:                  0
```

An invalid action or action outside the declared vocabulary must have an
explicit, pre-registered behavior. It may terminate the episode or consume a
turn, but different behavior creates a different environment and must not be
changed inside a comparison.

Lab 31 must publish one transition table for valid actions, repeated actions,
out-of-vocabulary words, and malformed output. The existing benchmark accepts
every alphabetic five-letter string, scores repeated guesses, and appends them
to history. A Part IV environment that rejects or terminates on any of those
actions has intentionally different dynamics. Compatibility tests therefore
cover only valid, non-repeated shared trajectories. The adapter must include
separate tests that document each intentional divergence before any legacy
result is compared with a Part IV result.

Candidate reduction, entropy, teacher agreement, format validity, and
history consistency are diagnostics in the first trajectory-RL experiments.
They are not silently added to the reward. A policy can maximize immediate
candidate reduction while failing to finish games, and a normal-mode probe may
be legal while not being a current answer candidate.

Later reward-shaping experiments may add a turn cost or another declared
signal. They must compare against the sparse-reward environment with the same
initial policy, decoder, action vocabulary, answer split, and model-call
budget.

## Lab 31: Define the Wordle environment

**Goal.** Turn the existing Wordle mechanics into one explicit environment
interface without changing the game rules.

**Work.**

* Implement reset and step around the existing scoring and candidate-filtering
  primitives.
* Define valid action, invalid action, repeated action, terminal win, terminal
  loss, and six-turn exhaustion.
* Freeze the policy observation schema and keep hidden environment data out of
  it.
* Add trace serialization and replay tests that reproduce every transition.
* Verify agreement with the established benchmark on the shared valid,
  non-repeated action domain.
* Add explicit transition tests for malformed, out-of-vocabulary, and repeated
  actions. Do not describe a different transition rule as a benchmark match.

**Lesson.** The environment owns transitions. The policy receives observations,
not privileged game state.

## Sampling-policy gate

The answer-constrained decoder from Labs 18b through 18d is a deterministic
argmax over full-string scores. It remains an important evaluation interface,
but it is not a policy-gradient data source. A greedy argmax provides neither
rollout variation for GRPO nor a normalized behavior-policy probability for an
actor loss or importance ratio.

Before Labs 37 through 40, Part IV must define one stochastic training policy
over the declared action vocabulary. Its design may use constrained token
sampling or a normalized distribution over complete legal-word scores, but it
must state:

* action vocabulary digest and tokenizer version;
* EOS convention and action parser;
* restriction or masking algorithm;
* temperature and all sampling parameters;
* normalized probability of the selected action;
* per-token probabilities when the update requires them;
* behavior-policy checkpoint and random seed;
* the evaluation decoder, which remains separate from the training policy.

The rollout trace must preserve these values and a replay test must recompute
the selected-action probability from a frozen checkpoint. An empirical sampler
test must also show that observed action frequencies agree with the declared
distribution on a fixed state suite.

Without this gate, a constrained rollout is useful for diagnosis but not for
policy-gradient training.

## Lab 32: Analyze policy-created states

**Goal.** Expand the Part III capstone's trace diagnosis into a reproducible
analysis of the states the policy creates.

**Work.**

Compare the capstone's expert and policy rollouts by:

* turn and remaining-turn distributions;
* candidate-count buckets;
* feedback patterns and duplicate-letter cases;
* repeated or invalid action states;
* state novelty relative to the supervised corpus;
* broad-state and sharp-state action quality;
* failure states that the teacher corpus rarely visits.

Use a fixed, policy-independent state suite as well as full games. Do not
compare later turns across policies as though they encountered the same states.

**Lesson.** Compounding error is a distribution problem. A small early error can
move the policy into a history that expert-only data never prepared it to read.

## Lab 33: Extend on-policy imitation

**Goal.** Turn the Part III one-pass correction experiment into a bounded,
iterative DAgger-style loop only when its result passes the Lab 30 entry gate.

Each iteration freezes the collector policy, collects new rollouts, persists
eligible visited states, queries the declared teacher, trains one fixed
supervised budget, and evaluates on the frozen answer set. At every iteration,
the static control receives the same number of newly labeled expert states,
matched by the Lab 30 answer-branch and state-stratum rule, and the same
cumulative update budget.

The candidate-only teacher, any Part III full-action teacher, and the deployed
decoder may have different action spaces. Every correction record must identify
which teacher produced its label and which decoder produced the visited state.

**Lesson.** On-policy data can be useful without allowing the policy to grade
itself. Iteration earns its cost only after the one-pass capstone shows a clear
reason to continue.

## Lab 34: Define state value targets

**Goal.** Define "better state" before training a critic.

Candidate count and feedback entropy are useful diagnostics, but neither is
automatically a value function. A singleton state illustrates the difference:
every guess has zero feedback entropy, yet one guess can finish the game.

This lab must choose and document one target for each value experiment, such
as:

* probability of solving within the remaining turns under a declared reference
  policy;
* expected turns remaining under that policy;
* optimal expected solve probability under a separately specified exact solver;
* rollout return under the current policy.

Each target answers a different question. The PRD does not call one "exact"
until the reference policy, state space, and computation are defined.

Compare the chosen target with simple baselines such as candidate count,
remaining turns, and the teacher's one-step entropy. Store target provenance
with every row.

**Lesson.** Reward records what happened in an episode. Value estimates what
will happen from a state under a declared policy.

## Lab 35: Train and validate a critic

**Goal.** Determine whether a learned value estimate is useful enough to
support policy updates.

**Work.**

* Train a critic against the Lab 34 target using only the approved policy
  observation.
* Evaluate error, rank correlation, calibration, and the ability to order
  independently held-out states.
* Compare against the simple value baselines from Lab 34.
* Evaluate both broad states and sharp late-game states. An aggregate error
  number can hide a critic that is accurate only where the policy already wins.

The critic's training split must remain separate from final gameplay answers.
If the critic is not better than a trivial baseline on the states that matter,
Part IV records that result and does not treat it as an advantage oracle.

**Lesson.** A critic is another learned model with its own failure modes. It is
not a reward function written in a different file.

## Lab 36: Assign credit across turns

**Goal.** Make temporal credit assignment visible before optimizing the policy.

For stored episodes, compute return, temporal-difference residuals, and
advantages from the declared critic and reward. Show how the same terminal
result changes the credit assigned to early and late guesses.

The action-observation boundary must stay explicit:

```text
action token(s) -> Wordle feedback -> next action token(s)
```

Environment feedback is not a policy token. The SAO paper's
[skip-observation GAE discussion](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.07508v1)
provides one design to study. Part IV must compare any such estimator with a
plain, declared alternative before claiming that it reduces noise in Wordle.

**Lesson.** A win or loss does not explain by itself which earlier action should
change.

## Lab 37: Actor-critic Wordle

**Goal.** Test whether critic-derived advantages improve a competent policy over
full model-generated episodes.

The actor receives the policy observation and samples an action from the
stochastic training policy defined by the sampling-policy gate. The critic
estimates the Lab 34 target. The update uses only complete persisted
trajectories with normalized behavior-policy probabilities.

Compare against:

* the frozen supervised entry policy;
* the best DAgger iteration when Lab 33 ran, otherwise the one-pass Lab 30
  `rollout_correction` arm;
* a terminal-reward policy-gradient control, if it fits the same compute
  budget.

Report policy drift, critic diagnostics, reward variance, legal-action rate,
singleton closure, whole-game solve rate, and turns on wins. Do not replace a
full-game metric with actor loss.

**Lesson.** Actor-critic learning is justified only if the critic makes
trajectory feedback more useful than sparse outcome alone.

## Lab 38: Revisit GRPO with the simulator

**Goal.** Compare Lab 12's completion-level formulation with full
environment-based groups.

For each fixed hidden answer and initial observation, sample a group of complete
Wordle episodes:

```text
same initial state and answer
-> sampled policy rollout A -> trajectory return A
-> sampled policy rollout B -> trajectory return B
-> ...
-> group-relative update
```

The policy never sees the answer. It only receives feedback after each action.
Keeping the hidden answer fixed inside a group makes the competing trajectories
comparable while preserving the policy's partial observability.

Match the initial checkpoint, action decoder, action vocabulary, answer sample,
sampling distribution, return definition, and model-call budget against the
other RL arms. Report group size and the fraction of generation time spent
waiting for the last rollout.

**Lesson.** GRPO can use a simulator. The question is whether its grouped
rollouts are worth their extra samples and synchronization cost for Wordle.

## Lab 39: Local single-rollout actor-critic

**Goal.** Study a local single-trajectory formulation that motivates SAO
without presenting it as an asynchronous SAO reproduction.

The first implementation remains local and bounded:

* collect one complete Wordle rollout;
* retain normalized behavior-policy log probabilities, checkpoint identity,
  action-distribution metadata, and collector seed;
* compute advantage with the declared critic;
* apply the declared policy update;
* measure policy lag between collection and update;
* compare one critic update per policy update with a separately declared
  faster-critic schedule.

This local loop normally has little or no policy lag. It is a single-rollout
actor-critic baseline, not evidence about SAO's asynchronous off-policy claim.
It may study skip-observation advantages when Lab 36 supports that comparison.

An asynchronous SAO-style arm is optional and has a separate gate. It must
induce and measure nonzero policy lag, retain normalized behavior-policy
probabilities, declare the importance-ratio and clipping or masking rule, and
compare against the local baseline under a matched model-call budget. The
MoE-specific frozen-attention technique in the SAO paper is out of scope for
this dense 0.6B model unless separately justified.

**Lesson.** Single-rollout updates trade grouping cost for a more demanding
value-estimation problem.

## Lab 40: Compare policy-learning methods

**Goal.** Compare data source and optimization structure under one evaluation
contract.

| Method | Training data | Full environment rollout | Critic | Grouped samples |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Supervised baseline | expert states | No | No | No |
| On-policy expert correction | policy states plus expert labels | Yes, for collection | No | No |
| Trajectory GRPO | policy trajectories | Yes | No | Yes |
| Actor-critic | policy trajectories | Yes | Yes | No |
| Local single-rollout actor-critic | policy trajectories | Yes | Yes | No |
| Optional asynchronous SAO-style arm | policy trajectories | Yes | Yes | No |

When Lab 33 does not run, the on-policy imitation row is the one-pass Lab 30
arm. When it does run, report both the Lab 30 arm and the best iterative result.

Every arm must use:

* the same eligible initial checkpoints;
* the same answer split and evaluation decoder;
* the same or explicitly normalized model-call budget;
* the same terminal reward definition;
* the same declared stochastic training policy where a policy-gradient update
  is compared;
* fixed seeds and persisted trace artifacts;
* a clear stop condition for MPS memory, wall-clock time, and invalid-action
  rate.

The final comparison reports gameplay, state-level diagnostics, critic quality,
policy stability, environment steps, model calls, training time, and compute
per additional solved game. It does not select a method solely by reward
curve.

**Lesson.** The right method depends on the feedback structure, value quality,
and compute budget. No method wins by acronym.

## Lab 41: Build the evidence-backed learning loop

**Goal.** Assemble only the mechanisms that passed their earlier controls.

The final system may contain:

```text
frozen policy baseline
-> rollout collection
-> distribution analysis
-> expert relabeling and/or value targets
-> approved policy update
-> held-out evaluation
```

It is not a promise of unconstrained self-improvement. The loop must retain
split boundaries, version every policy and dataset, and run held-out evaluation
before a new policy becomes the next collector.

The final report compares:

* the Part I completion-level RL baseline;
* the best Part II and III supervised baseline;
* the best imitation-based Part IV policy;
* the best trajectory-RL policy;
* the cost and failure modes of every improvement.

**Lesson.** A learning system improves only when its data, evaluation, and
update rules make a better next iteration more likely than a worse one.

## Experimental rules

Part IV inherits Part II's controlled-experiment discipline.

Every lab must state before training:

* hypothesis;
* intervention;
* fixed controls;
* state and answer splits;
* policy observation;
* action vocabulary and decoder;
* reward and terminal rules;
* compute budget;
* primary measurement;
* result interpretation for positive, null, and harmful outcomes.

Full-game evaluation is the primary capability measure. Action-level metrics
explain why a policy changed. Both are necessary.

Metrics should include:

* solve rate and turns on wins;
* legal-action, invalid-action, repeat, and history-consistency rates;
* action value, candidate reduction, and teacher disagreement where defined;
* state-distribution overlap and novelty;
* critic error, calibration, and advantage distribution;
* policy loss, KL drift, reward variance, and collapse indicators;
* environment steps, model calls, tokens, elapsed time, and MPS memory.

Training runs must use the existing MPS memory guard. Rollouts and scoring
should be restartable from persisted artifacts. A long-running experiment that
cannot identify its checkpoint, policy view, decoder, answer split, and trace
source is not evidence.

## What Part IV does not do

Part IV does not:

* rewrite the recorded Part II data and representation results;
* assume that the Part III full action space improves the model;
* expose hidden answers, future feedback, expert choices, or value labels to
  the policy;
* call one-step entropy a universal value function;
* introduce reward shaping without a sparse-reward control;
* build distributed rollout workers to imitate SAO's systems design;
* treat a result from one seed or one small answer battery as a population
  claim.

The teaching sequence remains deliberate:

```text
Part I: Learn the techniques.
Part II: Fix the supervised policy with evidence.
Part III: Test the action space, problem scale, and one controlled
          policy-state correction.
Part IV: Learn from the consequences of actions in an environment.
```

The final transition is from training a model that predicts plausible Wordle
words to training a policy that learns to play a sequential game.
